Who Connects the REDD+ Network? Brokers and Concentrated Polycentricity

A review of Gallemore and Munroe (2013), Centralization in the global avoided deforestation collaboration network. This post explains brokerage and examines the paper's three main network visualizations.

1. Research Context

REDD+ stands for Reducing Emissions from Deforestation and Forest Degradation. In this article, it provides the setting for a question about environmental governance: when many organizations work together, where are the important connections concentrated? Forest projects need funding, technical expertise, and local knowledge. These resources often belong to different organizations.

Gallemore and Munroe distinguish institutional decentralization from spatial decentralization. A system can contain many separate organizations while its key connecting organizations are headquartered in only a few cities. They call this pattern concentrated polycentricity. Their study concerns the historical development of the network through June 2012. (pp. 1199–1201)

Figure 1 provides the concept. It places dispersed centers, concentrated polycentricity, and a centralized hierarchy along a continuum. It is a stylized illustration, so making it involves arranging example nodes and links to explain different structures, rather than calculating values from project data. The three empirical network figures discussed below are the paper's original Figures 3, 4, and 5; Figure 2 introduces the growth of the dataset.

My interpretation: counting the organizations involved is only a starting point. I also need to ask how they connect to one another and where the organizations making those connections are based.

2. What Does “Broker” Mean?

The clearest definition appears in Section 2, on journal page 1201, which is page 3 of the PDF:

“Brokers are individuals or organizations who are positioned between two or more distinct groups in a network.”

Here, a broker is an actor that connects groups with different resources or knowledge. For example, an NGO might link an international funder, technical experts, and a local forest organization. Its connecting position creates opportunities to bring partners together and influence how information or resources move. The term describes a network position; it does not necessarily mean a paid intermediary or carbon trader. (pp. 1201–1202)

Where the relevant paragraphs are

These paragraph openings help locate the discussion in the article. Page numbers below include both the printed journal page and the PDF page.

  1. “Advantageous positions in the network skeleton…” — p. 1201 / PDF p. 3. This paragraph gives the definition and explains why REDD+ participants need brokers: funding, technical capacity, and local expertise are distributed across different groups.
  2. “Organizations with ties to places…” — p. 1201 / PDF p. 3. The Nature Conservancy illustrates brokerage. Its connections to American firms, forest project locations, and Washington, DC help bring together funding, expertise, and project sites.
  3. “Brokers might expect their position…” — p. 1201 / PDF p. 3. The authors suggest that organizations associated with widely adopted standards may become more attractive partners as those standards spread.
  4. “The implications of brokerage are ambiguous.” — p. 1202 / PDF p. 4. Brokers can support information exchange, coordination, and learning. They may also give their own views more influence than those of other participants.
  5. “Brokerage could generate other perverse incentives.” — p. 1202 / PDF p. 4. When connected groups build lasting direct relationships, they may depend less on their broker. The authors identify a possible incentive problem, rather than showing that specific organizations intentionally prevent direct cooperation.

My interpretation: a broker's importance comes from connecting groups that have something to offer one another. That position can help cooperation, but it also raises a question about whose priorities shape the exchange.

3. Data and Network Construction

The authors introduce a dataset of 276 projects and 763 organizations. The projects began on-the-ground activities between 1989 and June 2012. They include avoided deforestation, sustainable forest management, and REDD+ projects with an explicit carbon-reduction or REDD+ goal. An organization counts as involved if it implements, funds, or provides technical support for a project. (pp. 1199, 1204)

Figure 2 provides the growth context. Its two line charts show project numbers and total represented project area from 1995 to June 2012. Both grow rapidly after approximately 2005. To construct these charts, I would organize project dates and reported areas, summarize the represented projects and area for each year, and plot two aligned time series. The area chart describes project coverage in the dataset; it does not measure how much deforestation was prevented. The article does not specify every missing-area or overlap treatment. (pp. 1204–1205)

The collaboration network can be understood in three steps:

  1. Record project membership. Prepare project identifiers and dates, organization identifiers, organization types, and headquarters cities. Link each organization to the projects in which it participates.
  2. Create organization-to-organization ties. Two organizations are connected when they participate in the same project. An organization is a node, and a shared-project relationship is an edge.
  3. Count shared projects. If two organizations collaborate on several projects, their edge receives a larger weight. For historical snapshots, follow project timing and the authors' assumption that connections persist over a project's duration.

These steps reconstruct the workflow described in the methods. The original figures are reproduced below for discussion; I have not rerun the authors' dataset. A shared-project tie records documented co-involvement, rather than a directly observed amount of money, communication, or authority. (pp. 1204–1205)

4. A Related Technique: Betweenness Centrality

Betweenness centrality measures how often a node lies on the shortest paths connecting other pairs of nodes. It is useful here because an organization linking otherwise separated groups has opportunities to act as a broker. Having many partners and being between other groups are related but different properties.

For each pair of other nodes, an organization receives credit for the share of shortest paths passing through it. In compact notation:

CB(v) = Σs<t; s,t≠v σst(v) / σst

Here, σst is the number of shortest paths from node s to node t, and σst(v) is the number of those paths passing through node v. If several shortest paths are equally short, the credit is divided among them.

The authors normalize the score by its theoretical maximum for an undirected network with n nodes:

CBnorm(v) = CB(v) / [(n − 1)(n − 2) / 2]

This gives a value between zero and one. As a simple teaching example, suppose A connects only to B, and C connects only to B. B lies on the only path between A and C. Its raw score is 1 and, with three nodes, its normalized score is also 1. A and C score 0. This example is illustrative; it is not a result from the paper.

The actual project network is weighted. More shared projects mean a stronger connection. The authors use a shortest-path method that gives equal emphasis to connection strength and the number of steps along a path. Therefore, using shared-project counts directly as travel distances would misrepresent the method. Network distance here is also different from geographic distance. (Section 4.2, p. 1205)

A high score indicates an opportunity to connect groups. It does not directly demonstrate political control or successful forest protection. The authors also caution that normalization does not perfectly remove the effect of changes in network size.

5. Figure 3: Where Is Brokerage Concentrated?

What the visualization shows

The four maps show 2000, 2005, 2010, and 2012. Circles represent cities where participating organizations are headquartered. Larger circles indicate higher normalized betweenness in the city-level network. These are headquarters locations, not forest project locations. The map presents centrality values without drawing all the network's edges.

Results and interpretation

The network spreads across more places, but major connecting positions remain concentrated in a few cities, particularly in North America and Europe. Washington, DC remains the most prominent city through 2012. Paris and especially Geneva become more prominent after 2005. Jakarta is the prominent center the authors identify in a tropical forest country. (pp. 1205–1206)

The accompanying text reports that at least one of seven Washington-based organizations participates in about 22% of the projects in the dataset. It also reports that about 56% of the dataset's projects outside the United States involve a US-based organization. These percentages describe project involvement; they are not the centrality values encoded by circle size.

Steps for making this visualization

  1. Construct the weighted organization network for each selected year.
  2. Group organizations by headquarters city and sum the ties between each pair of cities.
  3. Calculate and normalize betweenness on the resulting city network.
  4. Join city identifiers to geographic coordinates and draw circles on a Robinson world map.
  5. Use the same size legend across the four year panels.

The order matters: aggregate the connections first, then recalculate centrality. Adding together the centrality scores of organizations in a city would produce a different measure. Changes over time should also be read with the paper's network-size caution in mind.

My interpretation: projects can become geographically widespread while access to important connecting organizations remains concentrated. This is the clearest visual example of concentrated polycentricity.

6. Figure 4: Which Types of Organizations Become Brokers?

What the visualization shows

This figure compares the distribution of organization-level centrality in 2012 across six types: academic organizations, firms, funds, governments, international organizations, and NGOs. The vertical axis is normalized betweenness.

Each violin shows where scores are concentrated within a category. The box shows the middle 50% of scores, with a line for the median. Whiskers follow the 1.5-interquartile-range rule, and dots mark values beyond them. Each violin has the same maximum width, so its width does not show how many organizations belong to the category.

Results and interpretation

Most organizations have centrality at or close to zero. The authors explain that most participate in only one project, while relatively few connect different project groups. Some governments and NGOs have especially high values. Firms are the largest category in the figure, with 287 organizations, but generally do not occupy the main brokerage positions. (pp. 1205–1206)

The international-organization group deserves care: it contains only four organizations, all with nonzero scores. Its broad violin therefore should not be read as evidence of a large sample. A low score also does not mean an organization is unimportant for local implementation; it means that it seldom sits on shortest paths between other organizations.

Steps for making this visualization

  1. Calculate centrality for every organization in the full 2012 network.
  2. Create a table with organization name, type, and centrality.
  3. Group the scores by type and estimate each group's distribution to form the violins.
  4. Overlay boxplots and label each category with its sample size.
  5. Scale the violins to equal maximum width, as stated in the original caption.

Centrality is calculated before grouping. Computing it separately inside an NGO-only or firm-only network would answer a different question.

My interpretation: the number of participants in a category does not tell me how much that category connects the wider network. A few organizations can connect many project groups even when most participants have a local focus.

7. Figure 5: How Do Individual Brokers Change Over Time?

What the visualization shows

This is a heatmap. Years from 1990 to 2012 run horizontally, and organizations run vertically. Darker cells represent higher normalized betweenness. The rows are the top 20 organizations ranked by their 2012 scores, traced backward through time. They are not a new set of twenty organizations selected each year.

Results and interpretation

The organizations occupying central positions change as the network grows. The United States government is ranked first in 2012, followed by The Nature Conservancy and the World Wide Fund for Nature. The latter began its first included project in 2007 and reached third place by 2012. Several organizations central in 2012 were not network participants as recently as 2005. (pp. 1206–1207)

The authors connect some of these patterns to cooperation between donors and large conservation NGOs. Their accompanying analysis reports involvement by a donor-country agency, the United Nations, the World Bank, or a foundation in 48% of projects involving five major conservation organizations, compared with 29% of other projects. The five are Conservation International, Fauna & Flora International, The Nature Conservancy, World Wide Fund for Nature, and Wildlife Conservation Society. This is supporting evidence from the text, rather than a value displayed in the heatmap. (pp. 1206–1207)

Steps for making this visualization

  1. Build each year's complete collaboration network and calculate centrality for all its organizations.
  2. Use the 2012 scores to select the twenty organizations to display.
  3. Retrieve those organizations' earlier scores from the full-network results.
  4. Arrange the data as organization-by-year cells and order rows by the 2012 ranking.
  5. Apply a common grayscale color scale and add a centrality legend.

The calculations must use the full network, not a network containing only the twenty displayed organizations. This retrospective selection also leaves out earlier important brokers that did not rank in the top twenty in 2012. A pale cell alone does not establish that an organization did not exist; it can reflect little centrality or no recorded participation.

My interpretation: Figure 3 and Figure 5 describe different kinds of stability. Important headquarters cities can remain central even while the particular organizations acting as brokers change.

8. What the Three Figures Establish

FigureQuestionMain finding
3: City mapsWhere are connecting positions concentrated?A small set of headquarters cities remains prominent as the network expands.
4: Violin and boxplotsWhich organizational types occupy those positions?A few governments, international organizations, and NGOs are important brokers; many participants have low centrality.
5: HeatmapWhich organizations are central, and how does that change?The leading organizations change over time, even though geographic concentration persists.

Together, the figures support the authors' idea of concentrated polycentricity. Many institutions participate, but a smaller set of organizations and cities holds important connecting positions. The authors argue that this creates questions about accountability and meaningful participation by people living where projects operate. Those political consequences are concerns raised by the analysis, rather than outcomes directly measured by the figures. (Discussion and Conclusion, p. 1208)

There are also limits to the evidence. Project documents may record organizational participation unevenly. The dataset assumes ties last through a project's duration and treats some large international organizations as single entities. Its sources may favor large transnational projects, and groups opposing REDD+ are not well represented in a network built around implementing pilot projects. These choices affect which connections become visible. (p. 1204)

9. Reflection

What I learned

My main takeaway is that participation and brokerage are different. An organization may do valuable work on one project without connecting the wider network. Another organization may matter because it links funders, technical experts, and local partners across several projects.

I also learned that the same collaboration data can answer different questions depending on how they are organized. A city map highlights geography, a distribution plot compares types of organizations, and a heatmap reveals change over time. The calculations and grouping choices have to match the question before I decide how the figure should look.

What I still want to know

I would like to compare these structural positions with interviews or records of actual decisions. Do organizations with high betweenness shape policy in practice? Do local groups gain more influence when they build direct relationships with one another? Those questions would help connect the network measures to the participation concerns raised by the paper.
